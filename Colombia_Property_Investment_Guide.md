# Buying & Renting Property in Colombia — A Step-by-Step Guide

**For:** A US investor putting ~**$700,000** into Colombian real estate — one house for personal/visit use plus **six small houses to rent out** on a mountainside.
**Prepared:** June 2026. **Status:** Research summary, organized for decision-making. **Not legal or tax advice** — the final section lists the professionals to hire, and you should not move money until they're engaged.

> **The one-sentence version:** Wire the dollars in the *legally registered* way (never via Wise/Remitly for a property purchase), **title the personal-use house in her own name to unlock the easy Resident visa**, hold the **six rentals in a Colombian company (SAS)**, and spend your optimization energy on the **US capital-gains sale** and the **bank's exchange rate** — that's where the real money is saved.

---

## 1. The Big Picture — How the Pieces Fit

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| How to move the money? | Sell at Schwab → **wire USD** (not pesos) to a Colombian bank → the bank converts to COP and **registers it as foreign investment** with Banco de la República. |
| Buy personally or via a company? | **Hybrid:** personal-use house in **her name**; the **six rentals in a SAS** (Colombian LLC-equivalent). |
| Best visa? | **R (Resident) Investor Visa** — she qualifies outright and skips years of renewals. |
| Rent long-term or short-term? | Short-term (tourist) earns more but is a **full taxed business** (19% IVA, tourism registry); long-term is simpler and IVA-free. Decision details in §5. |
| Where's the money saved? | **#1 US capital-gains timing**, **#2 negotiating the FX rate**, **#3 structure & not over-declaring capital**, **#4 deferring SAS dividends**. See §6. |

## 2. Recommended Ownership Structure — the "Hybrid"

**Hold the personal-use house personally (in her own name).**
- Qualifies her for the **investor visa** (the visa requires property *in her name*, not a company's).
- Simple; no corporate tax layer; if she doesn't rent it, no business tax at all.

**Hold the six rental houses in a Colombian SAS (Sociedad por Acciones Simplificada).**
- **Liability shield** — tenants/guests sue the company, not her personal assets.
- **Deduct real expenses** — depreciation, maintenance, property tax, management, accountant fees — so tax applies to a *much smaller* net figure.
- **Cleaner exit & inheritance** — sell or pass on *shares* rather than re-titling six separate properties through Colombian probate.
- A foreigner can own 100% of a SAS and be sole shareholder.

> **Why not put everything in the SAS?** Because then the *visa* falls back to the harder "business-owner" category (you must keep proving company activity every renewal). Keeping the personal house in her name gives her the easy Resident visa **and** the SAS benefits for the rentals.

## 3. The Step-by-Step Roadmap

### Phase A — Before any money moves (US side)
1. **Hire the team** (see §9): a Colombian immigration lawyer, a Colombian corporate/real-estate lawyer, a Colombian *contador* (accountant), and a **US cross-border CPA**. Engage the CPA *first* — the US tax on selling the Schwab investments is the biggest single number.
2. **Plan the Schwab sale for tax** (§6, lever #1): decide which lots to sell, whether to split across two tax years, and harvest any losses. *This can save more than every Colombian fee combined.*
3. **Gather & apostille US documents** (passport, financial proofs). Apostille is done by the issuing US state's Secretary of State (vital/notarized docs) or the US Dept of State (federal docs). Translate non-Spanish docs into Spanish.

### Phase B — Move the money the compliant way
4. **Sell the positions at Schwab.** The *sale* is the taxable event in the US, not the transfer.
5. **Wire USD from the Schwab brokerage account** (not Schwab Bank checking — it can't do international wires). Fee ≈ **$15 online / $25 phone**. **Send dollars, NOT pesos** — you want the Colombian bank to do the conversion and the registration.
6. **At the Colombian bank (an "IMC" — e.g., Bancolombia, Davivienda, BBVA):** instruct them in writing that the inflow is **inversión extranjera directa** — *en inmuebles* for the personal house, *aporte de capital* for the money going into the SAS. The bank files the **declaración de cambio**, which **auto-registers the foreign investment** with Banco de la República. **This registration certificate is what (a) lets her legally take capital/profits back out later and (b) is required evidence for the visa.**
7. **Negotiate the exchange rate** with the bank's trading desk (*mesa de dinero*) toward the official **TRM** rate (§6, lever #2). On $700K this is worth **$10K–$15K**.

### Phase C — Set up the company (for the rentals)
8. **Form the SAS** via the Cámara de Comercio (chamber of commerce). If she's abroad, she signs a **power of attorney (poder)** — apostilled — so a Colombian representative files for her. Steps: draft bylaws (private document) → reserve the name → get a **pre-RUT** from DIAN → register *matrícula mercantil* + pay *impuesto de registro* → finalize **RUT** → open a corporate bank account. ~1–3 weeks.
9. **Capitalize the SAS** with the registered foreign-investment funds and have it purchase the six houses.

### Phase D — Buy the property
10. **Close at the notary (escritura pública)** — deeds are always in pesos. Note: signing the deed does **not** register the foreign investment; the bank wire in step 6 does. Have **source-of-funds documents** ready for the bank's and notary's anti-money-laundering checks.
11. **Pay property closing costs** ≈ **1.5–2%** (notary ~0.3%, registration tax ~0.7–1%, legal).

### Phase E — The visa & ID
12. **Apply for the R Resident Investor Visa online** via the Cancillería **SITAC** portal (§4), attaching the Banrep registration certificate + the title (*Certificado de Tradición y Libertad*) in her name.
13. **Register for the cédula de extranjería** (foreigner ID) at Migración Colombia **within 15 days** of visa issuance.

### Phase F — Go live with the rentals
14. **Register the RUT** activity codes, set up **electronic invoicing**, and — *if renting short-term* — register each cabin in the **RNT (tourism registry)** and verify the land's **POT/uso-de-suelo** permits commercial lodging (§5).

## 4. The Visa — the Easiest Path

**Recommended: the R (Resident) Investor Visa via real-estate investment.**

- **Threshold:** foreign investment of **650 × monthly minimum wage ≈ $285K–$310K USD**. At $700K she clears it ~2.3×.
- **Why it's easiest:**
  - **Skips ~5 years of renewing "M" migrant visas** before residency — she lands at the residency tier directly.
  - **Fewest documents, cleanest evidence:** passport + property title in her name + the Banrep investment-registration certificate. (Unlike the business-owner visa, she does **not** have to keep proving ongoing company activity.)
  - **Broadest rights:** can run her business, sponsor dependents (spouse/children), path to citizenship.
- **The one rule:** the property used for the visa must be **in her own name**, which is exactly why the hybrid structure keeps the personal-use house personal. Title **at least ~$300K** in her name (the visit house, plus a cabin or two if needed) to clear the threshold; the rest goes in the SAS without affecting the visa.
- **Mechanics:** file online at **SITAC**; an immigration lawyer handles it for a few hundred dollars. Government fees are roughly **$55 study fee + ~$470–$540 issuance**. Cédula fee ≈ **COP $294,000**.

> Avoid the **business-owner (Socio/Propietario) visa** unless forced into it — lower investment bar but a renewal treadmill that requires continuously proving the company is economically active.

## 5. Long-Term vs. Short-Term Rentals — the Decision

Because there are **six units**, Colombia treats this as a **business either way** (six exceeds the 5-contract threshold that makes renting "mercantil") — so RUT, ICA, and e-invoicing apply regardless. The big differences:

| | **Short-term / tourist (Airbnb-style)** | **Long-term residential** |
|---|---|---|
| Tourism registry (RNT) | **Required**, renewed every Jan–Mar; fines 5–50 min. wages + closure if you skip it | Not required |
| IVA (VAT) | **19%** on nightly revenue (you'll exceed the small-operator exemption) | **Exempt** |
| ICA (municipal) | Yes | Generally no (yes if via SAS) |
| Tenant law | Hospitality contract — **free pricing, easy turnover** | Ley 820 — rent increases capped at inflation (**5.10% for 2026**), strong tenant protections, slow eviction |
| Gross yield | **Higher** | Lower |
| Admin burden | Heavy (RNT, IVA returns, guest reporting, cleaning/ops, likely staff) | Light |

**The gating item for mountainside cabins:** verify the land's **POT / uso-de-suelo** (zoning) actually permits commercial lodging *before* committing — rural/protected land can restrict it. This is the #1 due-diligence risk.

**Practical read:** Tourist renting usually wins on yield, but the **19% IVA + ICA + management** erode the advantage. Have the contador model both on realistic occupancy before deciding. A **mix** (some cabins long-term for stable base income, others short-term for upside) is common.

## 6. The Money-Saving Playbook (ranked by dollars saved)

**Lever #1 — US capital-gains timing on the Schwab sale (potentially the biggest saving).**
Selling ~$700K of investments can trigger a large US capital-gains bill. Ways to cut it:
- **Sell highest-cost-basis lots** (specific-lot identification) to minimize the gain.
- **Split the sale across two tax years** to stay in lower brackets (0%/15% vs 20%, and avoid the 3.8% NIIT surtax where possible).
- **Harvest losses** elsewhere in the portfolio to offset gains.
- Confirm everything is **long-term** (held >1 year) before selling.
- *There is no US–Colombia tax treaty,* so you can't lean on a foreign tax credit to smooth this — plan it on the US side. **Work this with the CPA before selling.**

**Lever #2 — Negotiate the USD→COP conversion (~$10K–$15K on $700K).**
- The bank's exchange spread is the biggest *transfer* cost. **Each 1% = $7,000.**
- Get **live quotes from 2–3 banks'** trading desks the day of conversion and push toward the **TRM**. Bancolombia is repeatedly cited as transacting near TRM.
- Ask about a **forward/limit order** to lock a good rate.
- **Do NOT** use Wise/Remitly/Xoom to fund a property purchase: they'd save ~1% but deposit *pesos locally*, **bypassing the foreign-investment registration** — risking your ability to repatriate $700K later and exposing you to channeling penalties **up to 200%**. The ~$7K "saving" can cost you everything.

**Lever #3 — Structure smartly & don't over-declare share capital.**
- The hybrid (personal house + SAS rentals) is itself a tax/liability optimizer.
- **Declare only the share capital the visa needs**, not the full property value — the *impuesto de registro* is **~0.7% of declared capital**, so over-declaring wastes up to ~$4,900. The SAS can *buy* the houses with capitalized funds rather than carrying a huge nominal share capital. (Lawyer/contador to set the figure.)

**Lever #4 — Defer the SAS dividend layer.**
- A SAS pays **35% corporate tax**, then distributing profit to a non-resident adds a **20% dividend withholding** (no US treaty relief) — ~48% if you strip all cash out yearly.
- **Retain/reinvest** profits in the SAS instead of distributing every year to defer that second layer; pull cash out in lower-income years.
- Maximize **deductible expenses/depreciation** to shrink the 35% base.

**Lever #5 — Manage the 183-day tax-residency line.**
- Spending **>183 days/year** in Colombia makes her a **Colombian tax resident — taxed on worldwide income** (up to 39%), not just Colombian income.
- If she has substantial US income, **staying under 183 days** can save significantly. (Doesn't affect visa eligibility — only tax.)

**Lever #6 — Smaller but real savings.**
- **GMF "4×1000":** the 0.4% transaction tax hits when paying the seller from the peso account (~$2,800 on $700K). A **cuenta de compensación** can avoid GMF on the channeled operation — ask the contador if it's worth it for a one-off.
- **Hold property >2 years** so the eventual sale is taxed at the **flat 15%** *ganancia ocasional* rate instead of ordinary rates.
- **Watch the currency trap on exit:** Colombian gains are computed **in pesos**. If the peso weakens, she can owe 15% on a *peso* gain even if she broke even in dollars — model this before selling.
- **Compare municipalities** for *predial* (property tax, ~0.5–1.6% of cadastral value) and ICA rates.

## 7. Tax Summary at a Glance

**US side:**
- Capital-gains tax on the Schwab **sale** (0/15/20% long-term + possible 3.8% NIIT). Moving the money itself is **not** taxed.
- **FBAR** (FinCEN 114) once her Colombian accounts exceed **$10,000** at any point in the year — informational, no tax.
- **FATCA Form 8938** may also apply (higher thresholds).
- Don't split wires to dodge reporting — *structuring* is a federal crime. (Normal bank wires aren't "cash" reports anyway.)

**Colombia side:**
- **No tax just to receive** her own money, but it **must be channeled & registered** as foreign investment (property purchase ≠ ordinary remittance).
- **Rental income:** non-resident ≈ flat **20%** on gross rent (long-term); resident = progressive up to **39%** on worldwide income. Via SAS = **35%** corporate (on net, after deductions) + **20%** dividend on distribution.
- **IVA 19%** on short-term lodging; **exempt** for long-term residential.
- **ICA** (municipal) on the rental business; **predial** annual property tax.
- **Ganancia ocasional 15%** on sale if held ≥2 years.
- She'll likely have to **file** a Colombian return (filing ≠ owing). Keep proof the funds are pre-existing savings.

## 9. The Professionals to Hire (don't skip — this is how you avoid five-figure mistakes)

1. **US cross-border CPA** — optimize the Schwab sale (lever #1, the biggest number) and handle FBAR/FATCA.
2. **Colombian immigration lawyer** — files the R visa via SITAC; confirms current thresholds/validity.
3. **Colombian corporate & real-estate lawyer** — sets up the SAS, structures the personal-vs-company split, runs title and **zoning (POT/uso-de-suelo)** due diligence, sequences the wire vs. closing.
4. **Colombian *contador público* (accountant)** — models personal-vs-SAS tax, short-vs-long-term rental, dividend timing, ICA/predial, and keeps the SAS compliant.

Their combined fees are small next to protecting $700K, preserving repatriation rights, and optimizing the tax — and several of these decisions (declared capital, residency days, sale timing) only pay off if set up *before* the money moves.

## 10. Key Risks & Things to Double-Check

- **Verify the live numbers before acting:** the peso/USD rate, the minimum-wage and UVT values (they reset every January and drive the visa thresholds and tax brackets), and the current visa fees.
- **Zoning is the biggest gating risk** for mountainside cabins — confirm commercial lodging is permitted on that land *before* buying.
- **The compliant transfer is non-negotiable** for a property purchase — registration is what protects the $700K.
- **No US–Colombia tax treaty** — income can face tax in both countries with limited smoothing; the CPA + contador should coordinate.
- **Currency-gain trap on exit** — Colombian tax is computed in pesos; model it.
